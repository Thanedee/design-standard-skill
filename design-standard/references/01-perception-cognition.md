# 1. Perception and cognition: why design works

Viewers judge a visual's appeal in about 50 milliseconds, and simple, familiar-looking layouts win that judgement. Every rule later in this guide follows from a handful of findings about how eyes and brains process images.

### 1.1 First impressions are near-instant and stable

- Lindgaard and colleagues showed web pages for 50 ms; appeal ratings at 50 ms closely matched ratings made after longer viewing ([Behaviour & Information Technology, 2006, reported by bit-tech](https://www.bit-tech.net/news/website_hot_or_not/1/)). **\[Research\]**
- Tuch et al. cut exposure to 17 ms. Pages with **low visual complexity and high prototypicality** (they look like what the viewer expects that kind of page to look like) were rated most appealing; complexity is judged first, prototypicality catches up within about half a second ([IJHCS 70(11), 2012](https://research.google/pubs/the-role-of-visual-complexity-and-prototypicality-regarding-first-impression-of-websites-working-towards-understanding-aesthetic-judgments/)). **\[Research\]**
- **Rule (SHOULD):** reduce clutter before adding polish, and respect the genre conventions of the format (a property listing should look like a property listing) unless breaking them is the point.

### 1.2 Processing fluency: easy to process feels beautiful and true

Reber, Schwarz and Winkielman argue that “the more fluently the perceiver can process an object, the more positive is his or her aesthetic response” ([Personality and Social Psychology Review 8(4), 2004](https://pages.ucsd.edu/~pwinkielman/reber-schwarz-winkielman-beauty-PSPR-2004.pdf)). **\[Research\]** Fluency rises with:

- **Less information** (more redundancy, fewer competing elements).
- **Symmetry**, especially vertical symmetry.
- **Figure–ground contrast and clarity.**
- **Repetition and familiarity** (the mere-exposure effect), which is why consistent series styling builds liking over time.
- **Prototypicality** (looking like a good example of its category).

Design implication: legibility is not only an accessibility issue; harder-to-read designs are also judged less attractive and less credible.

### 1.3 Gestalt grouping: the eye organises before it reads

The Gestalt principles, reviewed after a century of research by Wagemans et al. ([Psychological Bulletin 138(6), 2012](https://newiipdm.haifa.ac.il/wp-content/uploads/2015/05/Wagemans-Feldman-Gepshtein-Kimchi...-2012_PsychBulletin.pdf)), describe how viewers group elements without effort. **\[Research\]**

| Principle | What viewers do | Design use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Proximity | Treat close items as one group; grouping strength falls off steeply with distance | Space between groups must be clearly larger than space within them (at least 2:1) |
| Similarity | Group items that share colour, shape, size or weight | Give every item of the same kind identical styling |
| Common region | Group items inside the same boundary | Use cards, panels or tinted backgrounds to bind content |
| Connectedness | Group items joined by lines | Use connecting lines for steps and flows |
| Good continuation | Follow smooth lines and curves | Align elements along a clear axis to lead the eye |
| Closure | Complete incomplete shapes | Allows minimal icons and logos; never rely on it for critical information |
| Figure–ground | Separate an object from its background | Keep strong contrast between text and what sits behind it |
| Symmetry | See symmetrical regions as figures | Use symmetry for stability and formality |
| Common fate | Group things that move together | In motion design, animate related items together |

### 1.4 Attention follows salience, then goals

- Visual salience (contrast in colour, size, brightness, motion, isolation) captures attention first; the viewer's task then steers it. The strongest contrast on the canvas should therefore sit on the most important element.
- **Scanning patterns.** Nielsen Norman Group eye-tracking (2006, updated 2017) found an F-shaped scan on unformatted text, plus layer-cake (headings only), spotted (hunting a keyword), marking (eyes fixed while scrolling) and bypassing (skipping repeated first words of list items) patterns ([NN/g](https://nngroup.com/articles/f-shaped-pattern-reading-web-content/)). The F-pattern is the default “when there are no strong cues”; good hierarchy replaces it. **\[Research\]**
- **Rule (SHOULD):** front-load headings and list items with the most informative words; never begin every bullet with the same word.

### 1.5 Cognitive load and choice

- Working memory holds only a few chunks at once (cognitive load theory, Sweller, 1988). Group content into 3–5 chunks and remove anything that does not serve the message. **\[Research\]**
- Hick’s law: decision time grows with the number of options. One primary call to action per piece. **\[Research\]**
- Fitts’s law: targets are faster to hit when larger and closer. Applies to buttons and tap targets (section 14). **\[Research\]**

### 1.6 The aesthetic–usability effect

People rate attractive interfaces as easier to use. Kurosu and Kashimura (CHI 1995) found r = 0.59 between perceived aesthetics and apparent usability; Tractinsky, Katz and Ikar replicated it (r = 0.66 before use, 0.71 after) and found that aesthetics, not actual usability, drove post-use ratings ([Interacting with Computers 13(2), 2000](https://mycourses.aalto.fi/pluginfile.php/1645430/mod_folder/content/0/Tractinsky%20%282000%29.pdf)). **\[Research\]** Caveat: beauty buys tolerance, not a pass; a beautiful design that hides the message still fails.

### 1.7 Practical tests derived from the research

1. **Squint or blur test:** the main message and focal point must survive a heavy blur.
2. **Thumbnail test:** view at 20% size; the hierarchy must still read.
3. **Grayscale test:** hierarchy must not depend on colour alone.
4. **Five-second test:** a stranger shown the design for five seconds can state its main message.
5. **Genre test:** it still looks like the kind of thing it is (an invitation, a valuation report, a sale post).
